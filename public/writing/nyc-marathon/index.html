Where runners slow down in the New York City Marathon

October 8, 20265 min read

Last year 59,122 people finished the New York City Marathon, the most in the race’s history. This year’s race is on Sunday, Nov. 1.

Timing mats record every runner at each mile from mile 3 to the finish. I used the splits of the 58,721 finishers with complete records to see where runners slowed down, measuring each mile against the runner’s own average pace.

The race starts on the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge, the highest point of the course, and runners who finished near 4:30 ran the first three miles 7 percent faster than their average.

Through the flat miles of Brooklyn, they kept running 3 to 8 percent faster than their average.

They reached halfway, on the Pulaski Bridge, in 2:07:18, and the second half took them about 15 minutes longer.

The climb over the Queensboro Bridge was the first mile they ran slower than their average, about 22 seconds slower than mile 14.

On First Avenue they ran close to their average pace until mile 19, in East Harlem.

Mile 20, over the Willis Avenue Bridge into the Bronx, was 9 percent slower than their average, and miles 21 to 23 stayed about that slow.

The climb up Fifth Avenue in mile 24 was their slowest mile, 12 percent slower than their average.

They ran the rolling last two miles through Central Park, and the runner on the map finished in 4:29:12.

Scroll to run the race clock to 11:13 a.m., when the men’s winner finished and 14,976 runners had not yet started.

When the men’s winner finished, the last finisher was at mile 5.1, in Sunset Park, Brooklyn, and would be on the course for another 10 hours and 49 minutes.

By 6 p.m., 2,442 runners were left, spread over the last 8.5 miles of the course.

The last of the 59,122 finishers crossed the line at 10:01 p.m., 12 hours and 45 minutes after starting.

Who slowed down

Comparing each runner with their own average pace puts fast and slow runners on the same scale. Among runners under three hours, 85 percent ran the second half slower than the first, by a median of 3.0 minutes. Among runners who took five hours or more, 97 percent did, by a median of about 26 minutes.

Where the hills mattered

The biggest climb is the first one, about 140 feet up the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge, and runners ran it only about 1 percent slower than the flat miles after it. The Queensboro Bridge climbs about 100 feet, from mile 14.8 to 15.6, and runners ran miles 15 and 16 about 3 percent slower than miles 13 and 14. Fifth Avenue climbs about 90 feet in mile 24, which runners ran about 4 percent slower than the two miles before it.

Miles 21 to 23 have no hills, yet runners ran them about 7 percent slower than their average. The climb up Fifth Avenue came on top of that, which made mile 24 the slowest of the race.

About the data

Results and splits are from New York Road Runners’ public results for the 2025 TCS New York City Marathon. The official count is 59,122 finishers; the results list has 59,121 of them, and the counts on the race clock use that list. The pace figures use the 58,721 finishers with complete split records, counting a record as complete when no more than four of its 25 timing-mat times are missing.

Each mile is compared with the runner’s own average pace for the race. There are no timing mats at miles 1 and 2, so the first segment runs from the start to mile 3. The cards describe all finishers within five minutes of the chosen time, and the camera follows one of them, the runner whose pace by mile is closest to that group’s median.

Each dot starts at its wave’s scheduled time plus the gap between the runner’s gun and net times, and moves at an even pace between timing mats. A dot is gray within 2 percent of the runner’s average and fully colored at 10 percent faster or slower. The 400 runners with incomplete splits, most of them among the last to finish, are placed by even pace between the times that were recorded; the last finisher has no times after mile 14.

Buildings within 300 meters of the route are from the city’s Building Footprints dataset on NYC Open Data, drawn at their roof heights. The route is based on the 2023 course published on Strava and WNYC Data News’ course and mile-marker file, calibrated to the official miles. Elevation is from the U.S. Geological Survey’s 3D Elevation Program, with bridge decks at their modeled height. Land and borough lines are from the U.S. Census Bureau.

The published data has no names, bib numbers, runner IDs or hometowns.
